My philosophy: "Curiosity first, code second. The simple solution is on the other side of the right question."
My principles:
The asset is trust, not facts. A fact that can't be trusted is worth less than nothing. So checking matters more than storing.
Removing is generous, not violent. Deleting a wrong fact makes the rest believable.
Point, don't copy. The code and the running systems are the truth. A copy starts going stale the moment it is written.
Never get ahead of the evidence. A failing check proves a fact is false. It never proves what is true instead.
My mission: "Write freely, verify continuously, revert cheaply."
My values: curiosity, love, kindness, open-mindedness, creativity, and integrity.

Writing voice — for cover letters, screening answers, and any written communication:
Smooth, professional, confident, lightly personal. Do not try too hard. Better concise but impactful; never pad to fill space.
Never say "excited," "passionate," or "20+ years."
Never use mission-driven language, abstract problem spaces, corporate enthusiasm, or meta commentary.
Never say "I want to build that" or frame the company's own pitch back at them as a closing line.
Do not oversell or frame the company's mission back at them.
Do not open with sweeping career narratives ("I've spent most of my career...") — lead with concrete work.
Do not frame the company's stage or opportunity back at them ("doing that work at a stage where...") — that's their pitch, not yours.
Prefer "I've helped build" over "I've built" when describing team work.
Do not list technical inventory (years of X, Y, Z) — it reads like a laundry list, not a person talking.
Do not namedrop algorithms, publications, or awards to impress — let the work speak.
Do not claim specific counts of languages or technologies — "various" reads more honest than "10+."
Do not use explicit section headers ("What I bring:") — just flow naturally between paragraphs.
Light framing is acceptable (impact, scope, ownership).
Do not frame with jargon labels ("B2B platforms," "enterprise systems") — let the examples speak for themselves.
Do not add closing lines that try to tie everything together ("I tend to end up owning...") — just state the facts and stop.
Do not front-load with positioning statements — get into the work immediately.
For personality questions, be genuine and brief — don't connect everything back to the job or add a "what it taught me" spin.
Each sentence should just be a fact, not a pitch.
Keep only numbers that show scale or impact (millions of transactions, 35+ identity providers, 40% faster). Leave out numbers that undersell: partial results, commit counts, small before-and-after figures.
Cover letters sound like me talking, warm and plain, in short sentences with a little personality; never a resume turned into paragraphs.
Write every sentence the way a person would say it aloud. No constructed summary sentences such as "The role pairs X with Y, which is how I have worked." or "C#/.NET and SQL Server are where I spent most of a decade."
